Banking

Why banking diversification matters

A resilient international banking structure is designed around what you need it to do — not around how many accounts you can open.

Private client briefingApprox. 6 minute read

In this briefing

Most people only think about their banking structure when something goes wrong.

Where the risk appears

Until then, one bank can feel perfectly adequate. Your accounts work. Transfers clear. Cards function. There is no obvious reason to change anything.

The problem is that an international financial life rarely stays simple. You might move country, acquire another residency, start receiving income from somewhere new, restructure a business, sell an asset or make a large cross-border transfer. None of those things are inherently unusual, but they can change how a bank views you.

When a bank decides it needs to understand your circumstances again, the fact that you have been a client for ten years does not necessarily make the process easier. A compliance review can mean additional questions, delayed transactions or restrictions while information is being checked. In some cases, the bank may decide that your profile simply no longer fits the kind of client it wants to service.

The point

That is where having everything tied to one institution becomes a problem.

The objective is not more bank accounts

We do not diversify a client's banking simply for the sake of having accounts in different countries. We start with what the client actually needs their banking structure to do.

That might mean making sure a business can continue operating if one account is temporarily restricted. It might mean holding liquidity in more than one jurisdiction. It may involve keeping personal wealth separate from an operating company, gaining access to different currencies, or establishing a banking relationship in the country where a client expects to spend more time in future.

The structure comes from the objective.

Different institutions have very different appetites for international clients. One bank may be comfortable with a particular nationality but uncomfortable with the client's business activity. Another may accept the business but dislike the ownership structure. A third may be perfectly suitable if the application is presented through the right jurisdiction and supported with the right documentation.

Knowing those differences is where experience becomes valuable.

How we approach it

We work backwards from where the client needs to end up.

If the goal is to have reliable banking in two jurisdictions, for example, we look at the client's residencies, citizenships, businesses, source of wealth, expected transaction flows and future plans before deciding where those relationships should sit.

Sometimes the obvious route is the wrong one. There may be another institution that is more comfortable with the client's profile. There may be a better jurisdiction through which to establish the relationship. The ownership or operating structure may need to be arranged differently first. In some cases, one part of the wider strategy needs to happen before the banking application even begins.

Our approach

None of that is about disguising information or avoiding compliance. It is about understanding the rules well enough to structure a legitimate application around them rather than walking into the process in the least favourable way possible.

The advantage of having people on the ground

This is also where doing it alone becomes difficult.

Banking requirements are not always captured properly on a website or application form. What an institution is technically prepared to accept and what its relationship managers are currently comfortable onboarding can be two different things.

We work with people in the jurisdictions we operate in who understand those realities locally. That means we can find out how an institution is actually approaching a particular profile before a client spends weeks preparing an application that was unlikely to succeed in the first place.

If a bank is going to question a particular source of funds, we address it before submission. If an ownership structure is likely to cause unnecessary concern, we identify it. If another jurisdiction or institution is more appropriate for what the client ultimately wants to achieve, we change direction before the problem exists.

That is very different from simply handing someone a list of banks and wishing them luck.

Building a structure that works with you

The strongest international banking structures tend to look straightforward once they are finished. Getting them there is the difficult part.

A good structure should allow the client to move money when they need to, operate their businesses without unnecessary friction and avoid having their entire financial life dependent on one institution's changing risk appetite.

Our role is to understand how the different systems operate, identify the lawful routes available and put the pieces together in the order that gives the client the best practical outcome.

The principle

The point is not to fight the system. It is to understand it well enough that, as far as possible, the system works with you rather than against you.
