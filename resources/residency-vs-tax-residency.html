Residency is not the same as tax residency

A residency permit solves an immigration question. It does not, by itself, tell you where you are tax resident or what your wider tax position will be.

One of the most expensive mistakes in international planning is assuming that a residency permit tells you where you are tax resident. It does not.

Immigration status answers one question

Immigration residence and tax residence are separate legal concepts, even though they often involve some of the same facts: where you spend time, where your home is, where your family lives and where your business interests sit.

A residency permit generally answers: are you legally entitled to live in this country, and under what conditions?

That might be because of employment, investment, family relationships, financial independence or a particular immigration programme. It is primarily an immigration-law question.

Tax residence answers another

Tax residence is determined under the tax rules of the relevant jurisdictions. Those rules may look at physical presence, the location of your home and family, economic ties, longer-term legal concepts such as domicile and, in some cases, treaty provisions where two countries both consider you resident.

A 183-day threshold is common enough that people often treat it as the rule. It is not. Some jurisdictions can consider you resident before that point. Others apply additional tests. Day counts may also be calculated differently.

The correct analysis depends on the actual jurisdictions involved.

This is where strategy matters

If a client wants to relocate, acquire another residency or reduce their exposure to a particular tax jurisdiction, we do not begin by looking at a visa. We begin with the end position.

Where does the client want to live? Where will their family be? Where will the business operate? What income will continue to arise from the existing country? Which ties can realistically be changed, and which cannot?

Only then do we look at the residency route that supports that position. Otherwise it is very easy to solve the immigration problem while creating a tax problem.

Our approach

We work backwards from the position the client needs to reach, then coordinate the immigration route around the tax reality — not the other way around.

Sometimes the order matters more than the destination

International planning often comes down to sequence.

A client may need to change where they spend their time before a particular tax year begins. A home may need to be sold or retained carefully. A business relationship might need restructuring. Certain ties may need to be reduced before others are established elsewhere.

Doing those things in the wrong order can produce a very different result from doing exactly the same things in the right order.

We identify the position the client needs to reach, understand the domestic rules that apply in each relevant jurisdiction, then coordinate the steps required to get there lawfully.

Dual residence is where things become more complicated

It is entirely possible for two countries to consider the same person tax resident under their own domestic laws.

Where an applicable tax treaty exists, treaty residence may then need to be determined using the relevant tie-breaker rules. That can involve questions about permanent homes, personal and economic relationships, habitual residence and other treaty-specific factors.

This is not the kind of analysis that should be left to assumptions about where somebody has a visa or how many days they think they spent somewhere.

Knowing where the flexibility actually is

Different jurisdictions have very different approaches to residence and departure. Some rules are rigid. Others contain tests that depend heavily on facts and circumstances. Some positions can be improved significantly by changing how a client's affairs are organised before the relevant tax period begins.

That is where local knowledge becomes important. Our job is not to invent facts or disguise a client's position. It is to understand exactly how the rules operate and make sure the client's genuine circumstances are structured as intelligently as the law permits.

Sometimes that means a conventional route. Sometimes it means using an option another adviser would overlook because it sits outside the standard playbook.

The practical takeaway

A residency permit is not a tax strategy. The tax position has to be built separately, then coordinated with the immigration strategy so the two support each other.

We work with qualified professionals in the relevant jurisdictions to analyse the domestic rules and any applicable treaties, then coordinate the wider structure around the client's objective.
